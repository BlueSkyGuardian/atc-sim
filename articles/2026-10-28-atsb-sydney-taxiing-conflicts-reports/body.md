The Australian Transport Safety Bureau (ATSB) has published the last two of six preliminary reports into a cluster of occurrences at Sydney Airport (YSSY) in July and August 2026. Both new cases are taxiing conflicts on the ground, and both reports describe the staffing and rostering conditions in the Sydney control tower at the time, [ABC News](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2026-10-26/sydney-air-traffic-controller-high-fatigue-risk-report-says/107310362) and [Asian Aviation](https://asianaviation.com/posts/atsb-releases-last-2-sydney-ground-conflict-preliminary-reports) report. As with any preliminary report, they set out the sequence of events and the evidence gathered so far, with no findings or recommendations.

## 17 August: an incomplete readback

Virgin Australia flight VA1589, a Boeing 737, was taxiing to the domestic terminal after diverting from Newcastle because of bad weather. At about 09:40, the controller told the crew to follow a Qantas aircraft and to hold short of runway 25. According to ABC News, the Virgin captain read back only the hold-short part of the instruction. The controller did not notice the incomplete readback and moved on to other tasks.

The crew of the Qantas 737, QF420, which was vacating the runway, saw the Virgin aircraft approaching a taxiway intersection at a speed that suggested it would not give way. They stopped using normal braking, and the Virgin jet passed in front of them. The Virgin crew did not realise there had been a conflict until the Qantas crew queried it with the controller.

The report describes the controller's situation in detail. Rostered to finish at 20:50 the evening before and to start again at 05:30, the controller was the only one in the tower and had a high predicted fatigue level. The ATSB stresses that a predicted fatigue level does not mean a controller was actually fatigued, only that the roster created the risk; the controller described themselves as "fine and alert". They told investigators that equipment in the tower cab limited their view of the intersection, and they were removed from duty for the rest of the day. ABC News adds that the report notes that Airservices Australia, the national air navigation service provider, does not have a fatigue management system approved by the Civil Aviation Safety Authority (CASA).

## 25 August: pushback onto an active taxiway

Eight days later, a controller cleared Jetstar A320 JST745 to push back from bay 59 onto a taxiway. About 30 seconds later the same controller cleared QantasLink Dash 8 QLK427D to taxi along that taxiway, past the A320's bay. The Dash 8 crew saw the A320's tail moving backwards and stopped, about 100 metres from the bay; the pushback driver and dispatcher also stopped and warned the A320's crew. After both crews queried the controller, the A320 was towed back onto its bay and the Dash 8 passed behind it.

Three controllers told the ATSB that pushbacks from some gates are prone to conflicts because aircraft push straight onto an active taxiway rather than onto an apron, and the tower shift manager and a supervising instructor described the area as a possible "black spot". The shift manager was working under supervision while their endorsements were being reinstated, three trainees were on shift, and the controller on duty said they had "not had many days off in the past month". This investigation will also look at the tower's view of the taxiway and the systems available to controllers.

## Six occurrences, one common question

The first four preliminary reports, released on 30 September, included the most serious event: on 27 July a breakdown of coordination led a controller to clear a Qantas Boeing 737 (QF124) to cross a runway while a QantasLink Bombardier was landing. The 737 stopped short of the intersection of runways 34L and 25 but within the runway surface, and the two aircraft ended about 120 metres apart. The ATSB classified it as a category A runway incursion, the most severe, and only the third of its kind in Australia since records began in 2003, [ABC News reported at the time](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2026-09-30/nsw-atsb-sydney-airport-runway-conflict-preliminary-reports/107210322). Four of the seven controllers in the tower that day had high predicted fatigue risk.

ATSB Chief Commissioner Angus Mitchell said "an important safety defence acted appropriately" in the two latest cases, since flight crews and ground staff caught the conflicts, but that the bureau must establish why their actions were needed to prevent a more serious outcome. He added that the ATSB has not yet determined whether staff resourcing contributed to any of the six occurrences, or whether it was a condition that Airservices was managing effectively. Airservices said it would review the findings once the investigations are complete.

## Lessons for every tower

None of this is unique to Sydney. Readback and hearback errors, conflicting clearances given seconds apart, blind spots from the tower cab and fatigue from short rest between shifts are all classic threats in aerodrome control. The ATSB says it is reviewing the common factors across the six cases so that any systemic safety issue can be raised early. The final reports, with findings and analysis, will follow when each investigation is complete.
