Six weeks after it was switched on around Washington, the US Federal Aviation Administration's AI-supported traffic management tool has its first published results. On **30 October 2026** the US Department of Transportation (DOT) [republished a New York Post report](https://www.transportation.gov/briefing-room/must-read-dot-ai-program-easing-flight-traffic-control-tower-headaches-still-dems) saying that in its initial deployment the tool, called **SMART**, had helped **reroute 110 flights**, saving **an average of more than 10 minutes and 56 gallons of fuel per flight**, with some flights saving **up to 30 minutes**, and benefiting **14,644 passengers**.

## What SMART is, and what it is not

SMART stands for **Strategic Management of Airspace, Routes and Trajectories**. According to the [FAA's announcement of 21 September](https://www.faa.gov/newsroom/trumps-transportation-secretary-sean-p-duffy-delivers-state-art-air-traffic-control), it combines **about 200 data streams** (weather, flight paths, traffic flow and controller staffing among them) into a single platform, where an AI-supported engine forecasts conditions and recommends solutions before problems appear. The tool is supplied by **Air Space Intelligence**, which, according to [Nextgov/FCW](https://nextgov.com/artificial-intelligence/2026/09/dc-area-airports-first-deploy-ai-tool-airspace-management/416114), won a 12-year, **$875 million** FAA contract in June to provide SMART and flow management data services for a modernised Air Traffic Control System Command Center.

It is a **traffic flow management tool, not a separation tool**: it works on airline schedules, weather, airport capacity and airspace constraints to predict traffic flows, as Nextgov/FCW describes it, not on the real-time conflicts that controllers resolve. The FAA says its staff review every SMART recommendation and that the leadership of the local facility can accept or reject each one; SMART "cannot replace controllers or take control of an aircraft". Secretary Sean Duffy said in September that it would be driven "not by the most complicated AI, but AI that will provide information to them", and that it would help to "speed them up or slow them down" so that arrivals reach Reagan National in one-minute increments.

## Where it runs

Limited operational use began on **21 September 2026** in the airspace of the US capital. Nextgov/FCW names the three airports concerned: **Washington Reagan National (DCA), Washington Dulles (IAD) and Baltimore/Washington (BWI)**. The FAA plans to extend the tool gradually to other parts of the country over the coming months.

"Instead of leaving air traffic to deal with all these issues, we are actually getting ahead of issues," Rebecca Guy, the FAA's acting chief technology officer, told the New York Post. FAA Administrator Bryan Bedford said in September that "combining SMART with our ongoing hardware upgrades gives us the tools to cut flight delays". Those hardware upgrades are part of a modernisation programme for which the administration says **$12.5 billion** is being deployed for telecommunications, radios, radars and electronic flight strips, with completion targeted for the end of 2028.

## Not everyone is convinced

The tool remains politically contested. The DOT's post quotes a letter from Democratic critics saying such efforts are no "substitute for rebuilding the human-directed air traffic controller workforce", and notes that Representative Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez urged the FAA to stop the rollout. The FAA, for its part, says it is still recruiting: about **4,000 controllers** entered the training pipeline in the administration's first 18 months, and some **11,000 controllers** now work in its facilities.

## Read the numbers with care

The results come from a single place, the DOT's republication of a newspaper article, and cover a short initial period. They have not been published as a formal evaluation, and the post gives neither the method used to measure the time and fuel saved nor the number of flights handled in the area over the same period. For flow managers and controllers, the interesting point is less the headline figures than the model: a predictive tool that proposes reroutes before congestion or weather builds up, with the final decision left to the human traffic management unit. Whether that model holds up as SMART spreads beyond Washington is the next thing to watch.
