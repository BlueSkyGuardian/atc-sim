One year to the day after its first flight, NASA's X-59 quiet supersonic research aircraft left its desert test base and set off across the United States. The Lockheed Martin-built jet departed Edwards Air Force Base in California on Wednesday 28 October, bound for NASA's Kennedy Space Center in Florida, with refuelling stops in El Paso and Fort Worth, Texas, and at Birmingham, Alabama, according to [AIN](https://www.ainonline.com/aviation-news/aerospace/2026-10-30/x-59-heads-fl-nasa-marks-first-flight-anniversary), [Bham Now](https://bhamnow.com/2026/10/30/experimental-supersonic-nasa-jet-lands-in-bhm-along-cross-country-flight/) and [AvBrief](https://avbrief.com/x-59-heads-to-florida-from-edwards/). It is the aircraft's first long trip away from its home airspace.

## Four legs across the country

On Thursday 29 October the X-59 took off from Naval Air Station Joint Reserve Base Fort Worth at 09:48 local time and landed at Birmingham-Shuttlesworth International Airport at 11:03, a leg of 539 nautical miles, [Avi-go](https://avi-go.com/news/articles/77bf94dfd9521a791b9640a3aaaeb66b) reports. The final leg to Florida was expected on 30 October. [FLYING](https://www.flyingmag.com/nasa-supersonic-x59-kennedy-space-center-florida/) writes that the aircraft was due to land at the Space Florida Launch and Landing Facility (KTTS), the former Shuttle Landing Facility at Kennedy. None of the sources we read had yet confirmed the arrival.

NASA test pilots Nils Larson and Jim Less are flying the trip, with F-15 and F/A-18 chase aircraft alongside, AIN says. The X-59 has no forward-facing cockpit window: its pilot sees ahead through a camera-based eXternal Vision System (XVS), one of several unusual features of the design, which also include a very long, slender nose and an engine mounted on top of the fuselage, FLYING notes.

The reason for the trip is NASA's MAX POWER aerospace expo and air show at Kennedy Space Center. [AIAA](https://aiaa.org/2026/10/30/nasas-x-59-supersonic-jet-makes-first-cross-country-flight/), Bham Now and Avi-go give its dates as **7 and 8 November**; FLYING mentions flyovers on 8 and 9 November and says it is not yet clear whether the X-59 will fly or stay on static display.

## A year of envelope expansion

The ferry flight caps a busy first year. On its first flight, on 28 October 2025, the X-59 flew for 67 minutes at 12,000 feet and about 230 mph (370 km/h). Since then it has completed more than 40 flights, reaching **more than 56,000 feet and Mach 1.53**, according to NASA figures quoted by AIN and Avi-go.

The first supersonic flight came on 5 June 2026, and on 12 June the aircraft reached Mach 1.4 at 55,000 feet, the cruise condition planned for its noise measurements. In September it flew six times in three days, a record pace for the programme, FLYING reports; its longest flight so far lasted 102 minutes.

## What comes next: listening to the "thump"

The X-59 is the centrepiece of NASA's Quiet SuperSonic Technology (Quesst) mission. Its shape is meant to turn the sonic boom into a soft "thump" of about **75 decibels**, against 120 to 130 decibels for Concorde, FLYING says. NASA now moves from envelope expansion to **acoustic validation**, measuring the noise the aircraft actually produces, planned later in 2026 at Edwards. "This is the phase we've been working toward," said Larry Cliatt, the Quesst acoustic validation technical lead, in September.

After that come **community surveys**: flights over selected towns while residents report what they heard. FLYING describes plans for up to five 30-day studies in different regions. NASA will give the results to US and international regulators, who are considering whether to replace the ban on civil supersonic flight over land, in force in the United States since 1973, with a noise-based standard. The Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) proposed interim noise-based certification standards in June, according to FLYING.

## Why it matters to controllers

Today, supersonic flight over land is confined to military areas. If the Quesst data lead to a noise standard, controllers could one day handle civil aircraft climbing to around 55,000 feet and cruising at Mach 1.4 over populated regions, well above most of today's traffic and with very different climb and descent profiles. That future is still years away. This week's trip was a smaller step: an experimental aircraft with no forward window, escorted by chase jets, routed through busy civil airspace and onto a commercial airport, an exercise in coordination for the air traffic services along the way.
