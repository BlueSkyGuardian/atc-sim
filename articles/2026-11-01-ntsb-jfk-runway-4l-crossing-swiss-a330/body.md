Three words spoken between two controllers in the same tower cab were enough to send four aircraft across an active runway in front of a departing widebody. In its final report on a runway incursion at New York JFK on 17 April 2024, the US National Transportation Safety Board (NTSB) blames "nonstandard crossing phraseology and ineffective coordination" between the two local controllers, as reported this week by [FlightGlobal](https://www.flightglobal.com/ops-safety/2026/10/clearance-mix-up-allowed-four-jets-to-cross-jfk-runway-in-front-of-departing-a330/), [Aviacionline](https://www.aviacionline.com/english/accidents-and-incidents/ntsb-poor-coordination-between-controllers-forced-a-swiss-a330-to-abort-its-takeoff-at-jfk_a6ae618f99b2d9a633c6481b5) and [Aviation A2Z](https://aviationa2z.com/index.php/2026/10/31/ntsb-reveals-how-three-words-put-five-aircraft-in-danger-at-jfk-airport/). Aviation A2Z dates the report's release to 29 October; the investigation file is DCA24FA164.

## What happened

JFK was using the parallel runways 4L and 4R. Departures used 4L, under local controller 2 (LC2); arrivals landed on 4R, under local controller 1 (LC1), and then had to cross 4L to reach the terminals.

The Swiss Airbus A330-300, flight SWR17K to Zurich, was the next departure on 4L. LC2 asked LC1 to hold the crossings until the Swiss jet had gone and named the taxiways concerned: Yankee Alpha, Golf, Fox and Hotel. LC1 replied: "Roger, behind the departure, I'll go one at each."

LC2 meant the Swiss departure. LC1 understood "the departure" to be a Delta A321 that had just been cleared for take-off from 4L ahead of it. LC1 then began clearing the arrivals across.

According to the timeline in the report, as given by Aviation A2Z (local time):

- **16:45:41**: LC1 clears Delta 29, a Boeing 767-400ER, to cross 4L at taxiway Hotel;
- **16:45:50**: LC2 clears SWR17K for take-off;
- **in the next ten seconds or so**: LC1 also clears American 2246 (Boeing 737 MAX 8), Republic 5752 (Embraer 175) and Delta 420 (Airbus A220-100) to cross;
- **about 16:46:10**: the A330 begins its take-off roll;
- **16:46:15**: the Swiss crew reports rejecting the take-off.

The A330 stopped from about **50 knots**. The closest aircraft, the Delta 767, was crossing at about 14 knots roughly **4,692 feet (1,430 metres)** ahead. Nobody was injured and no aircraft was damaged.

## The findings

The NTSB points to two procedural failures, according to Aviation A2Z and Aviacionline:

1. **The phrase itself was prohibited.** JFK's own procedures say: "Do not use phraseology such as 'one at each.'" Crossings coordinated between local positions must name the runway and the crossing point for each aircraft.
2. **The condition was not read back.** A controller receiving a conditional coordination must repeat the limiting condition. LC1 did not, so neither controller discovered that "the departure" meant two different aircraft.

The coordination was spoken across the tower cab rather than over an interphone line, so it was **not recorded**; the investigators relied on the controllers' accounts.

The airport surface surveillance system, ASDE-X, did not alert. The A330 never entered the system's "departure" state, which requires set acceleration and speed thresholds, so no conflict was predicted. The NTSB found that ASDE-X worked as designed. In its coverage, Aviacionline also lists broader runway-safety recommendations, among them a review of the activation logic of runway status lights and cockpit traffic alerting for new and retrofitted aircraft.

## Why it matters to controllers

The lesson goes beyond JFK. A conditional clearance or coordination is only safe when both sides are certain which aircraft the condition refers to. ICAO procedures (PANS-ATM, Doc 4444) allow conditional clearances only in a strict form that identifies the aircraft or vehicle concerned, and require the condition to be read back. The same discipline applies to coordination between two positions in the same room, where it is easy to believe that a quick verbal exchange is understood.

Two practical points stand out for every tower unit that splits its local control between two positions: **name the aircraft, not "the departure"**, and **read back the condition**, even face to face. Recording coordination between positions also matters: without it, investigators, and the unit's own safety management, depend on memory.

The incident also shows the limits of surface safety nets. ASDE-X and similar systems are built to avoid nuisance alerts, which means they may stay silent until an aircraft is clearly accelerating. In this case, the Swiss crew's decision to stop at 50 knots closed the gap that procedures and technology had left open.
