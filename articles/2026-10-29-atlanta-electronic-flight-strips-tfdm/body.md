The controllers in the tower at Hartsfield-Jackson Atlanta International Airport (ICAO: KATL) have stopped writing on paper. The airport, one of the busiest in the world, has switched from paper flight progress strips to **electronic flight strips**, becoming the **26th airport** in the United States to adopt the technology, [WSB Radio reported](https://www.wsbradio.com/news/local/atlanta-airport-gets-major-air-traffic-control-upgrade-faa-replaces-decades-old-system/YR6ZCLJM3BHCVLMFNL3UVDMHMU/) on 29 October 2026. The Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) plans to install the system at **89 airport towers by the end of 2028**.

## What changes in the tower

The electronic strips are part of the FAA's Terminal Flight Data Manager programme (TFDM). According to the [FAA's description of TFDM](https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/technology/tfdm/whatistfdm), the system replaces paper strips with electronic ones that update the controllers' displays automatically with the latest flight data, and it shares that data among controllers, traffic managers, airlines and airports.

[Federal News Network](https://federalnewsnetwork.com/technology-main/2026/03/after-2025-collision-air-controllers-at-reagan-get-new-tools/), which reported earlier this year on the installation at Washington Reagan National (KDCA), describes how it looks on the position. Controllers can still move, order and annotate the strips, as they did with paper, but they also see an overhead map of the airport showing aircraft on the runways and taxiways, can tap a strip or an aircraft for more detail, and can send messages to aircraft. TFDM, developed and deployed by the FAA with the contractor Leidos, also helps sequence departures and arrivals so that aircraft do not wait unnecessarily.

WSB lists the expected benefits at Atlanta: real-time flight information, better monitoring of aircraft and coordination of ground movements, and quicker adjustments when the weather changes or a runway closes.

## A programme that is speeding up

The pace of the deployment can be read from the two reports. Washington Reagan National was the **15th** airport to receive the system, according to Federal News Network, which said the FAA had accelerated that installation by 45% and that twelve more airports were planned for 2026. Atlanta is now the **26th**. The remaining step to the target is large: 63 more towers to equip in a little over two years to reach 89 by the end of 2028.

The upgrade is part of the FAA's **$12.5 billion** air traffic control modernisation programme, which WSB says also covers radar, radio and network upgrades at major airports. It comes after a series of near misses, equipment outages and other incidents that have drawn attention to the age of the US air traffic control system.

## Beyond the strips

TFDM is more than a digital strip board. The FAA describes three other functions: surface collaborative decision-making, a real-time schedule of arriving and departing aircraft that supports departure metering on the airport surface; links to the FAA's traffic flow management tools, Time Based Flow Management (TBFM) and the Traffic Flow Management System (TFMS), so that aircraft can be managed from departure gate to arrival gate; and the consolidation of several older tower systems into one.

## Why it matters to controllers

Paper strips have survived for decades because they work: a strip is a physical memory aid that a controller can move, mark and pass to a colleague. Electronic strips keep those gestures but add what paper cannot: the data on every strip is shared, so the ground controller, the tower controller and the traffic management unit see the same, current information, and a change in one place appears everywhere.

For Moroccan controllers and trainees, the US rollout is a useful reference. Many European towers have used electronic strips for years; the FAA's programme shows the same transition on a very large scale, in some of the world's busiest towers, and with the same aim: fewer manual tasks, shared data and better management of departures on the surface.

## What comes next

The FAA's target is 89 towers by the end of 2028. Neither report lists the next airports in line.
