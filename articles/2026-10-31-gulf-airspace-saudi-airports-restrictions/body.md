Weeks after the attacks that closed Riyadh's King Khalid International Airport (OERK), air traffic in Saudi Arabia is still far from normal, while the big Gulf hubs a few hundred kilometres away keep operating. A status report published on 30 October by [International Business Times](https://www.ibtimes.com.au/middle-east-airports-riyadh-flight-restrictions-1877624) and an update of 29 October by the travel site [Wego](https://blog.wego.com/saudi-arabia-airspace-flights-status-affected-airlines-what-travelers-need-to-know/) describe the picture airlines and air navigation service providers are working with.

## Riyadh: every movement needs permission

Riyadh is operating on a limited basis. Under a Notice to Airmen (NOTAM), which Wego identifies as **A3650/26**, every arrival and departure needs prior permission from Saudi Arabia's General Authority of Civil Aviation (GACA). Saudi airlines are flying from Terminal 1 (domestic) and Terminal 2 (international) since 25 October, but normal scheduling has not resumed and most foreign airlines have not returned.

The two sources differ on the date of the reopening: Wego says the airport reopened on 18 October, with Saudi carriers resuming on 25 October, while IBTimes speaks of a partial reopening announced on 25 October.

The scale of the disruption is visible in the schedules. According to Cirium data quoted by IBTimes, about **86% of scheduled departures** from Riyadh between 8 and 25 October were cancelled or did not fly. Air France has suspended Riyadh until 12 December and Cathay Pacific until 31 January 2027; Etihad has cancelled its Riyadh and Dammam flights until 5 November and KLM until 27 November. Emirates plans to resume around 8 November and British Airways expects a limited service from 3 November. Both sources stress that a published date does not guarantee a restart.

## The other Saudi airports

- **Jeddah (OEJN)**: open, with intermittent disruptions and cancellations; some foreign operations are moving to a newly phased Terminal 4, according to IBTimes.
- **Dammam (OEDF)**: open again after an attack on 21 October; the authorities say traffic has returned to normal, with intermittent delays and continuing cancellations.
- **Abha, Jazan and Najran**, in the south near Yemen, operated under temporary closure notices in October, some at night only; IBTimes reports that some restrictions were lifted late in the month. Wego advises checking the current NOTAM.

The cause is the series of attacks by Yemen's Houthi movement on Saudi targets in October, including the airports of Riyadh and Dammam. Saudi defences reported intercepting missiles and drones over the Riyadh area again on the evening of 29 October.

## The Gulf hubs keep flying

Across the water, the picture is different. Dubai International, Hamad International in Doha and Zayed International in Abu Dhabi were operating normally or with minor delays on 30 October, IBTimes reports; Bahrain and Muscat reported no closures, and the airspace of the United Arab Emirates is open to commercial flights. In Kuwait, terminals were working, but some airspace movements required prior approval.

## What the European regulator advises

The European Union Aviation Safety Agency (EASA) keeps a set of Conflict Zone Information Bulletins (CZIBs) for the region, valid until **16 November 2026**, as [Avi-Go](https://avi-go.com/news/articles/ca06a4019e5652b0c3496a130bab663c) reported when they were renewed on 30 September. They include one for the Persian Gulf and the Gulf of Oman and, since 30 September, a new bulletin, **CZIB-2026-09**, for a defined part of the **Jeddah flight information region (FIR, OEJD)**; Wego says it was revised on 9 October (R1) and recommends avoiding the specified parts of the FIR and taking care elsewhere. The bulletin for Iran advises against flying in the Tehran FIR at any level.

## Why it matters to controllers

Bulletins like these move traffic. Flights that avoid parts of the Jeddah FIR or the Gulf take longer routes through neighbouring airspace, and EUROCONTROL's latest forecast says the Middle East crisis will remove about 110,000 flights from European traffic this year (see our separate article). The next milestone is **16 November**, when EASA is due to review its bulletins for the region.
