SpaceX has told US regulators that the main constraint on how often it can launch is no longer rocket production but the way airspace is managed around launches. The company made the point in a 24-page comment dated 26 October, the last day of a consultation by the US Department of Transportation (DOT) and the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) on new spaceports and "priority airspace" for launch corridors, according to [Avi-Go](https://avi-go.com/news/articles/7010235cd56921c6f7e6f29f7f4b1fa0) and [Tesla North](https://teslanorth.com/2026/10/28/spacex-faa-airspace-rules-cap-10000-launches/), which both reported the filing on 28 October. The airlines' trade association answered in the same docket the same day.

## What the FAA asked

The consultation is a request for information (RFI), not a rule. Published in the [Federal Register](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/08/25/2026-17290/siting-new-spaceports-and-use-of-priority-airspace-for-critical-space-launch-corridors) on 25 August (docket FAA-2026-9736), it follows the 2026 National Space Transportation Policy (NSTP), which tasks the DOT to "designate priority airspace for critical space launch corridors". The FAA notes that it does not currently use the term "space launch corridor". Today, launch operators work with the FAA's Air Traffic Organization to set up **aircraft hazard areas** (AHAs) for each launch, and the RFI asks how corridors would differ from that, how they would be governed and how they could limit the impact on airspace efficiency.

The numbers behind the question are large. According to the RFI, the United States carried out 217 of the world's 329 launches in 2025, more than half of them from Cape Canaveral and the Kennedy Space Center. The FAA expects activity to reach **10,000 FAA-licensed launches and reentries a year by 2035**, going from a few launches a week to several a day, and the NSTP sets a goal of more than 1,000 launches and reentries a year by 2030.

## What SpaceX asks for

According to both reports, SpaceX says it alone expects to carry out 10,000 launches a year, against 182 FAA-licensed launches and 7 licensed reentries in 2025 (the figure the filing uses; the RFI's 217 counts all US launches). Its planned Starbase spaceport in Louisiana is designed for about 30 launches and reentries a day.

The company argues that the current process closes too much airspace for too long: Avi-Go reports that airspace closures now average just over two hours per launch, while the FAA's corridor concept expects a normal vertical launch window of under three minutes. SpaceX asks for:

* priority for its missions inside a corridor;
* automatic airspace approval within 48 hours of a request;
* a waiver of airspace fees;
* integration of its Space Data Integrator and "Cleared" launch-status platform into the FAA's traffic-flow tools, so that hazard areas can be smaller and move with the vehicle.

SpaceX says it already provides Cleared and its programming interface free of charge to most major US airlines.

## The airlines' answer

Airlines for America, whose members include American, Delta, United, Southwest, FedEx and UPS, filed comments on the same day, Avi-Go reports. Citing the FAA's own forecast, it expects serious operational impacts from a rise to 10,000 launches a year, and asks that new spaceports be assessed for their effect on existing airspace users and that full modernisation of air traffic control be delivered in parallel.

## Why controllers should care

Launch and reentry windows are already part of the job for controllers near the US launch coasts: hazard areas are activated, traffic is rerouted or held, and the airspace is returned once the vehicle is clear. Avi-Go also recalls that failures of SpaceX's Starship have scattered debris across busy routes and disrupted flights. Moving from about two hundred launches a year to thousands would turn an occasional event into a daily one, which is why both the operators and the airlines frame the question as one of air traffic management.

None of this is decided. As Tesla North points out, the filing approves nothing: licensing, environmental reviews and integration into air traffic control all remain to be done. With the comment period now closed, the next step belongs to the FAA and the DOT, which have not said when they will decide whether and how to define launch corridors.
