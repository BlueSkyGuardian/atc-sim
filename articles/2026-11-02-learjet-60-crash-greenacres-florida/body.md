A Learjet 60 business jet carrying nine people crashed into a residential area of **Greenacres, in Palm Beach County, Florida**, at about **12:50 local time on Sunday 1 November 2026**, killing everyone on board. The aircraft, registered **N722BD**, had taken off from **Austin, Texas (KAUS)** and was bound for **North Eleuthera in the Bahamas (MYEH)**, according to the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA), as reported by [ABC News](https://abcnews.com/US/learjet-9-aboard-crashes-palm-beach-florida-faa/story?id=137753599). US Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy confirmed that all nine occupants died.

## A descent of more than 26,000 feet in about a minute

The FAA's figures, quoted by ABC News, show the jet descending **from 35,250 feet to 8,750 feet in a little over a minute** before it struck the ground near Military Trail, between Cresthaven Boulevard and 10th Avenue North. Video shared after the accident shows the aircraft hitting the ground nose-first and bursting into flames; the impact left a crater close to a shopping centre.

Flight-tracking data described by [BizJet Journal](https://bizjetjournal.com/business-aviation-news/learjet-60-n722bd-crash-palm-beach-florida) and by an account syndicated on [Yahoo News](https://www.yahoo.com/news/weather-news/articles/footage-shows-learjet-falling-35-230749686.html) show the jet cruising at about 41,000 feet over the Florida peninsula, then descending to about 35,000 feet and levelling off briefly before the final, much steeper descent. BizJet Journal's own provisional reconstruction of the ADS-B (automatic dependent surveillance-broadcast) data suggests a tightening right-hand spiral lasting about 77 seconds, but the publication stresses that its speed and angle figures are estimates, not flight recorder data.

## What the crew told air traffic control

Recordings published by LiveATC, quoted by both outlets, give the outline of the last minutes on frequency:

- the pilot reported **ice accumulation, turbulence and an outside air temperature of minus 47°C**;
- according to the account citing CNN, the crew also reported precipitation and asked for a higher level, and the controller cleared the aircraft to **37,000 feet**;
- the crew then declared an emergency with a "Mayday" call and reported a rapid descent;
- in a later transmission, quoted by BizJet Journal, a pilot said: "We are in an overspeed condition descending 20,000 feet per minute."

Thunderstorms were reported in the area, but the Yahoo News account notes that it was not known whether the weather played a part. No cause has been established.

## The aircraft and the investigation

The FAA registry lists the Learjet 60 under serial number 271, registered to **LR60271 LLC of Wilmington, Delaware**, according to BizJet Journal; ABC News describes it as a 22-year-old twin-engine jet belonging to a private company and used in the past as a rental. Neither the operator nor whether the flight was private or charter had been confirmed at the time of writing. No injuries on the ground were reported, according to local broadcaster WPTV, quoted by Yahoo News.

The **National Transportation Safety Board (NTSB)** leads the investigation, with the FAA in support. The NTSB usually publishes a preliminary report about 30 days after an accident, and its final report with a probable cause 12 to 24 months later.

## Why it matters to controllers

Accidents like this one are a reminder of how quickly a high-altitude upset can unfold: in the FAA figures, the jet lost more than 26,000 feet in roughly the time it takes to read back a clearance. The first reports from the crew, of icing, turbulence and precipitation at cruising level, are exactly the kind of information that controllers relay to other aircraft in the sector and pass on as pilot reports. Until the NTSB publishes its findings, however, the sequence of events and the role of the weather remain unknown, and the figures quoted by the media on the descent rate vary from source to source.
